### Voice Actor

Voice acting requires interpreting a script or line and making vocal choices that serve the intended context.

Areas I pay attention to include:

- **Interpretation:** Understanding the purpose, subtext, and audience of a line before delivering it.
- **Pacing and pauses:** Managing timing so information is clear and the delivery feels appropriate to the scene.
- **Diction and articulation:** Keeping words intelligible without making delivery sound unnatural.
- **Vocal variation:** Adjusting pitch, energy, emphasis, rhythm, and tone to suit the line and intended character or context.

### Voice Coach

My voice-coaching approach is practical and goal-oriented. Feedback should be specific enough to act on, respectful of the speaker's natural voice, and tied to the purpose of the performance.

Coaching priorities can include:

- Clear articulation and understandable word endings.
- Pacing, pausing, rhythm, and phrasing.
- Tone, emphasis, expressive range, and vocal intention.
- Projection and vocal presence appropriate to the setting.
- Matching delivery to the script, audience, and intended effect.
- Reviewing a second attempt to identify what improved and what needs further practice.

The exact coaching plan should depend on the speaker's goal and the requirements of the task. I do not treat one accent, pitch range, or speaking style as universally superior; the relevant standard is fit for purpose, intelligibility, and the agreed rubric.

## Speech Assessment Framework

For a speech or voice-evaluation task, I would follow the project's instructions and rubric and use a consistent review structure.

### 1. Vocal Delivery

Describe perceived pitch range, speaking rate, rhythm, pauses, intonation, articulation clarity, and audible changes in energy or emotional tone.

### 2. Voice Quality

Comment on audible qualities such as breathiness, raspiness, resonance, or phonation only where they can be judged reliably from the clip.

### 3. Language and Pronunciation

Note pronunciation patterns and potential accent or dialect cues when the sample provides enough evidence. If evidence is limited, record uncertainty rather than over-identifying the speaker.

### 4. Delivery Context

Distinguish features consistent with read or scripted delivery from spontaneous or conversational speech, while recognizing that these are judgments, not certainties.

### 5. Recording Quality

Document audible background noise, reverberation, distortion or clipping, changes in level, and apparent distance effects.

Do not claim a specific microphone, headset, phone, or room type unless the audio provides a reliable basis.

### 6. Evidence and Confidence

Tie judgments to audible cues, use consistent terminology, document uncertainty, and separate observation from inference.

## Audio-Quality Review Checklist

When listening directly to a recording, I would check and document the following.

- **Intelligibility:** Are words and phrase endings understandable throughout the clip?
- **Background noise:** Is there audible hum, hiss, traffic, keyboard noise, or another competing sound? Does it mask speech?
- **Reverberation:** Does the voice sound dry or noticeably reflected by the room? Describe the audible effect without asserting an exact room type.
- **Distortion and clipping:** Are peaks harsh, flattened, crackling, or otherwise distorted?
- **Level consistency:** Does the loudness vary unexpectedly across words or phrases?
- **Distance and position:** Are there audible signs that the speaker moves nearer to or farther from the microphone? Treat the cause as tentative unless clear.
- **Signal limitations:** Is the sample too short, noisy, compressed, or incomplete to support a confident conclusion?

## Quality-Control Rubric

I would apply the project's rubric consistently. The following general scale can be adapted to the exact project instructions.

| Dimension | What a strong assessment demonstrates |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Descriptors match what is actually audible; no vocal or technical trait is invented. |
| Evidence linkage | Important judgments are connected to specific vocal or recording cues. |
| Terminology | Language is precise and consistent, avoiding vague labels where a more specific description is supported. |
| Confidence calibration | Uncertain accent, emotion, demographic, or equipment claims are appropriately qualified or omitted. |
| Recording observations | Noise, reverberation, distortion, and level variation are described separately from assumptions about their source. |
| Usefulness | The report is concise, easy to compare across samples, and relevant to the task objective. |

Suggested rating anchors:

- **1 —** Unsupported or materially inaccurate.
- **3 —** Broadly relevant but incomplete or too general.
- **5 —** Precise, evidence-linked, consistent, and well-calibrated.

Final scoring should always follow the official project rubric rather than this illustrative scale.

## Working Principles

- Describe the recording, not the speaker's presumed personality or identity.
- Identify accent or dialect only when there are sufficient cues and the task requires it.
- Separate what is heard from possible explanations for what is heard.
- Do not equate natural conversational features with defects unless the task criteria justify that decision.
- Use consistent labels across clips and follow reviewer guidance.
- Preserve confidentiality and avoid sharing recordings or project information outside authorized systems.
- Escalate unclear cases instead of forcing a precise conclusion from weak evidence.
